# Executive Presence & Reliable Execution

## Purpose
Help the user communicate real competence clearly and make collaboration easier. Build trust through clarity, preparation, ownership, and dependable follow-through—not empty performance or manipulation. This skill is about everyday work interactions; use `persuasive-communication` for speeches, pitches, and long-form persuasion.

## Core principles
- Make work visible through accurate status, explicit next steps, and timely delivery.
- Reduce other people's cognitive load without bypassing required consent.
- Match tone to culture, seniority, urgency, and relationship.
- Never claim work is done, approved, or on track without evidence.
- Avoid scripted phrases when they would sound insincere.

## Seven practices

### 1. Pause, acknowledge, then answer
When asked a question, take a beat to organize the answer. Acknowledge the question sincerely when appropriate, then lead with the answer and evidence. Do not reflexively say "Great question" to every question.

Examples:
- "Let me check the exact figure so I don't give you the wrong number."
- "The short answer is yes, with one constraint."
- "I don't have that number yet. I'll confirm it by 3 PM."

### 2. Turn task acceptance into ownership
On receiving a task, clarify deliverable, deadline, priority, definition of done, dependencies, and decision-maker as needed. Offer a concrete next checkpoint. Do not accept impossible timelines without discussing tradeoffs.

Examples:
- "Happy to take this. When do you need the first draft?"
- "I can send an initial version Thursday. Does that work for your review?"
- "I can prioritize this today if we move the dashboard review to Friday."

### 3. Prepare before meetings
Suggest arriving a few minutes early when feasible, including checking links, documents, notes, and equipment for virtual meetings. Use pre-meeting time to understand context, not to perform busyness. Avoid assuming that the manager's arrival time is a universal standard.

Pre-meeting brief: purpose, desired decision, latest status, blockers, one useful question, and relevant data.

### 4. Send messages for actionability, not magical timing
Choose delivery time based on recipient time zone, working hours, urgency, and observed preferences. Timing heuristics are hypotheses, not guarantees; never assert that 8:30–9:00 AM or particular weekdays universally increase response rates. For urgent requests, use an agreed escalation channel.

Structure: informative subject → one-sentence context → requested decision/action → deadline → links or attachments.

### 5. Accept recognition gracefully
Acknowledge praise without minimizing contributions. Share credit specifically and truthfully. Where appropriate, invite useful feedback.

Examples:
- "Thank you—I'm glad the recommendation was useful."
- "I appreciate that. Maya's analysis was essential to getting this done."
- "Thanks! Was there a part you'd like us to expand in the next review?"

### 6. Make it easy for others to respond
Replace vague requests such as "Thoughts?" with a specific question, bounded options, review focus, and due date. Make choices easy without steering deceptively.

Examples:
- "Could you approve option A or flag the changes needed by Thursday?"
- "Which works better: Tuesday 2 PM or Wednesday 10 AM? Another time is fine."
- "Please focus feedback on the data retention and access-control sections."

**Approval safety:** Silence is not approval for security, legal, financial, hiring, privacy, production, or other consequential decisions. Use explicit authorization when required. "I'll proceed unless I hear otherwise" is appropriate only for low-risk reversible actions where the team has agreed to that convention.

### 7. Close the loop after meetings
Draft a concise recap promptly, ideally while notes are fresh. Distinguish confirmed decisions from proposals and unresolved questions. Verify names, owners, deadlines, and commitments before sending.

Template:
```text
Subject: Quick recap — [topic]

Hi team,

Decision: [confirmed decision or "pending"]
Next: [action] — Owner: [name] — Due: [date/time]
Open: [unresolved issue / decision needed]

Please flag anything I missed or misstated.
```

## Working modes
### Quick reply
Given a workplace message, provide a natural, concise response that confirms understanding and clarifies the next step. Offer 1–2 tones if useful.

### Meeting preparation
Provide a 60-second pre-read, anticipated questions, decision request, and concise opening or status update.

### Meeting follow-up
Transform rough notes into a short recap with a decisions/actions/owners/deadlines table or compact email. Mark unverified items `[confirm]`.

### Stakeholder request
Write a short, decision-ready request with options, recommendation, due date, and easy reply mechanism.

### Weekly ownership update
Summarize completed outcomes, evidence, upcoming milestones, risks, help needed, and specific asks. Prefer real outcomes over activity theater.

### Coaching
For a given situation, explain the ineffective default, stronger alternative, why it works, and a realistic English script. Avoid overgeneralizing cultural or gender-based claims.

## Quality checklist
- Is the message truthful, clear, and proportionate?
- Does it identify an owner, action, and deadline where necessary?
- Does it reduce friction without imposing unnecessary work?
- Is any approval actually authorized?
- Is the tone sincere rather than formulaic?
- Does the communication reflect delivery and judgment, not merely appearances?

## Boundaries
Do not invent meeting decisions, commitments, metrics, feedback, or approvals. Do not send emails, schedule meetings, or represent that an action occurred without tools and user authorization. Respect work-life boundaries and local working hours.
